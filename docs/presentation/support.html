4 · Slide 2 — Proposed Solution

Three bullets fit comfortably. Below are ready-to-use versions, each defensible.

1 — What it does. DepthWizard turns a single RGB image — JPEG, PNG or GeoTIFF — into a metric Digital Surface Model and an explorable 3D flythrough in the browser. No stereo pair, no LiDAR flight, no InSAR pass.
2 — How it gets real metres. A frozen 97.5 M-parameter monocular depth backbone supplies relative depth; a lightweight 1.72 M-parameter trainable head converts it to absolute height above ground, learned from 7 804 LiDAR-verified tiles. Adding a Copernicus GLO-30 terrain datum yields absolute elevation.
3 — What makes it accurate. Per-pixel scale and shift rather than one global constant; a composite loss that keeps building edges sharp and explicitly weights tall structures; and GSD normalisation so a 0.25 m aerial photo and a 1 m satellite scene are presented to the model at the same scale.

The one-sentence version, if you need it

"A single satellite photograph, in, and a measured 3D city you can fly through, out — at 5.50 m RMSE against airborne LiDAR on terrain the model has never seen."

Why a judge should care about the design choice

The instinctive approach is to use a metric depth model, which outputs metres directly. We tried that: metric models assume a perspective camera with a focal point, and a satellite orthomosaic has neither — every pixel looks straight down. Correlation with true height was 0.06, effectively zero. A relative-depth model keeps working (0.41), because ordering "this is higher than that" survives the projection change. Learning the metres separately, from LiDAR, is what makes the system work at all. This is the single most interesting technical point in the project and it belongs in the deck.

How it addresses the problem statement

Innovation and uniqueness

1 — We use a relative-depth model, not a metric one, and we can prove why. The obvious choice outputs metres directly. It fails here: metric models assume a perspective camera, and a nadir orthomosaic has no focal point. We measured correlation with true height at 0.06 — indistinguishable from noise — against 0.41 for a relative model. Learning the metres separately from LiDAR is the design decision the whole system rests on.
2 — Per-pixel scale and shift, not one global constant. A single scale factor cannot be right for a roof and the street beside it. The head predicts a scale and a shift at every pixel from the DPT feature pyramid.
3 — A loss that knows tall things matter. Standard SILog on log1p quietly under-weights exactly the structures the task is about: a 60 m error on a tower scored barely 3× a 0.5 m kerb error. We added a height-weighted term (w = 1 + h/10) and measured the gain in a controlled ablation — 5.68 → 5.50 m. This is our own contribution, not something taken from a paper.
4 — One model, many sensors. GSD normalisation means the same weights serve a 0.25 m drone orthophoto and a 1 m satellite scene, with upsampling capped at 3× so we never invent detail.
5 — We ship the ground truth next to our output. Three demo scenes include the airborne-LiDAR surface of the same ground, on the same grid, in the same viewer. Anyone can fly through both and judge for themselves. Almost nobody volunteers this.
If only one innovation fits on the slide, use #1 or #5. #1 shows engineering judgement backed by measurement; #5 shows confidence. Both are memorable in a way that "we used a transformer" is not.

The five stages, in words

  1. Ingest & normalise. Read the image and its CRS. Compute true ground sample distance — including the linear_units_factor, because a CRS in US survey feet would otherwise produce heights 3.28× too large. Resample to the 0.33 m training GSD (upsampling capped at 3×).
  2. Relative depth. Frozen DA2-Base over 512² tiles with Hann-blended seams, so a gigapixel orthomosaic is processed without seam artefacts and without exceeding GPU memory.
  3. Metric calibration. The trainable DPT-pyramid head predicts a per-pixel scale and shift; nDSM = ReLU(scale · depth + shift) gives height above ground.
  4. Terrain datum. Copernicus GLO-30 is fetched automatically and grey-opened to bare earth; DSM = DTM + nDSM gives absolute elevation. Ground control points can correct any residual offset or tilt.
  5. Outputs. GeoTIFF DSM/DTM/nDSM, hillshade, 16-bit height map, and a textured GLB that the browser loads for the flythrough.

Loss function — and why the last term exists

L = 1.0·SILog(log1p) + 0.5·GradientMatching(4 scales) + 0.1·L1 + 0.5·HeightWeighted

  • SILog on log1p — scale-invariant, and log1p keeps it finite at 0 m ground.
  • Gradient matching at four scales — penalises blurred edges, which is what keeps roofs looking like roofs instead of hills in the 3D view.
  • Height-weighted L1, weight 1 + h/10 — ground counts 1×, a 10 m house 2×, a 20 m block 3×. Without it the objective barely cared about buildings: the log compressed a 60 m error on a tower to roughly three times the penalty of a 0.5 m error on a kerb, though it is 120× larger in metres. Adding it moved benchmark RMSE from 5.68 m to 5.50 m and cut under-prediction bias from −0.64 m to −0.24 m.

Engineering details worth one line each on the slide

  • 512² sliding-window tiling, Hann-blended seams — handles 8K+ imagery on ordinary GPUs.
  • GSD normalisation to 0.33 m — one model serves 0.25 m aerial and 1 m satellite imagery.
  • Shipped head is 6.6 MB; the backbone is downloaded once and frozen. Edge-deployable.
  • Graceful degradation: every optional component (vehicle prior, segmentation, GCPs) is absent-safe.

Say the limitation out loud; it reads as rigour. Buildings and trees carry 94.5% of all remaining error, and both are under-predicted (−6.7 m and −6.0 m bias). This is not a tuning failure — a single nadir view does not contain the parallax that fixes absolute height. We proved it: four independently trained models (different loss, different capacity, 2× the data) land within 0.35 m of each other on any given site, while the spread between sites is 15.5 m. The scene determines the error roughly 44× more than the model does.

10 · Appendix B — What we tried, and what it taught us

Three controlled experiments were run on identical settings, each changing exactly one thing. The ship rule was fixed before any of them ran, so a marginal result could not be argued into a win afterwards.

RunThe one changeRMSEOutcome
baseline—5.68 mprevious shipped model
E1Height-weighted loss5.50 m Shipped. The only change that improved the graded metrics.
E2Unfroze the last 4 transformer blocks (+28 M trainable)5.69 m No effect — and would have grown the shipped artefact from 6.6 MB to ~390 MB.
E32.05× the training data5.75 m No gain on RMSE — but the best median error (0.361 m vs 0.468 m) and best ground accuracy of all four models. More data improved typical accuracy, not outliers.
The finding worth presenting. We changed the objective, the capacity and the data volume — and only the objective mattered. The model was never short of parameters or examples; it was optimising a score that barely cared about the pixels carrying most of the error. That is a much stronger statement than three unrelated numbers, and it is the kind of reasoning a technical judge rewards.

Hypotheses we tested and rejected

  • Overfitting. Ruled out: validation error was still falling at the last epoch, more data is the remedy rather than the cause, and a 1.72 M head against 16 004 tiles is nowhere near a capacity-limited regime.
  • Stale training labels. The LiDAR is 2020–2022 and the imagery 2023, which looked like a clean explanation for an accuracy floor. We re-ran three sites with epoch-matched 2022 imagery: two of three got worse. Rejected.
  • A capacity ceiling. Trained the head on just 24 tiles seen 60 times; it plateaued at 1.38 m and could not reach zero. Two nadir pixels can look identical at different heights, so the model is forced to average. The limit is information in the image, not model size.

11 · Appendix C — Known limitations (state these before you are asked)

  • Tall structures are under-predicted — building bias −6.7 m, tree bias −6.0 m. A single nadir view lacks the parallax that fixes absolute height.
  • Dense high-rise is the hardest case — Rotterdam centre 16.69 m RMSE, against 22.92 m for predicting nothing. We help, but the canyon geometry is genuinely ambiguous from above.
  • Flat farmland is the one place we can lose to doing nothing — 1.59 m vs 1.43 m for predict-zero. Cause identified: large bright concrete aprons read as roofs, a direct artefact of training data that is 87% urban. Three blobs account for 23% of that site's error.
  • Benchmarked on Dutch terrain. AHN is the best open LiDAR available to us, but the Netherlands is flat and Northern-European. Indian urban form and real relief are untested, and we say so rather than implying global validation.
  • No error bar yet on 5.50 m. Run-to-run spread between four models was 0.35 m, so the 0.18 m gain over the baseline is real but modest. A seed-repeat is running.

12 · Appendix D — Likely questions, with answers

QuestionAnswer
"How can one image give height? Isn't that impossible?" The absolute scale is not in the image — it is learned. The backbone gives relative ordering ("this is higher"); the head converts that to metres using patterns learned from 7 804 LiDAR-measured tiles: shadow length, texture gradient, apparent size, context. Where the image is genuinely ambiguous, we under-predict, and we publish that bias.
"Why not a metric depth model that outputs metres directly?" We tried. Metric models assume a perspective camera; an orthomosaic has none. Correlation with true height was 0.06 — effectively zero. Relative depth survives the projection change at 0.41.
"5.50 m sounds large." It is dominated by tall structures. Ground and low vegetation — most of any image — are sub-metre (0.87 m and 0.95 m). The alternative of predicting nothing scores 8.37 m. And no single-view method resolves a 100 m tower's height from one nadir look; we quantify the gap rather than hide it.
"Have you tested on ISRO imagery?" Not yet — we have no ISRO LiDAR ground truth to score against. What we have tested is resolution robustness: performance is flat from 0.25 m to 1 m and degrades gracefully at 2 m, which spans the Cartosat range. The pipeline reads any georeferenced GeoTIFF.
"What happens with no GPU?" It runs on CPU. run.sh detects hardware and installs the right PyTorch build.
"Is this reproducible?" Every number in this pack comes from a JSON file in the repository, and a script (check_report_numbers.py) verifies the documentation against those files and fails if any figure drifts.
