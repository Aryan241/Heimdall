0:00 – 0:35 · The problem

Do: Open with Washington DC — civic loaded in the 3D flythrough. Orbit slowly once. Touch nothing else.
Elevation data is the first thing you want after a flood or an earthquake, and the first thing you do not have. A LiDAR survey is a commissioned flight — weeks and a budget. What you usually do have is a photograph.

This is DepthWizard. One RGB image in; the surface in metres out — buildings, terrain, canopy — as a scene you can fly through. No stereo pair, no LiDAR.
Avoid "AI-powered" and "state of the art". The people watching build satellites. Show a measurement and they trust you; use marketing language and they stop listening.

0:35 – 1:15 · Upload something real

Do: New processing job → drag valkenburg_hills__UPLOAD_THIS.tif in → confirm the GSD override is empty → Process image.
A real aerial GeoTIFF of a hill town in South Limburg — 400 metres across at 25 centimetres per pixel, one of eight sites we hold out. Never seen in training.

No preprocessing, no parameters. It carries its own coordinate system, so the pipeline reads the scale from the file.
Do: Hover Reference DEM and Ground control points so they are on camera. Leave both empty.
Both optional — one control point is worth about half a metre on absolute elevation. I am leaving them empty to show the default: everything it needs, it fetches itself.

1:15 – 2:15 · How it works, while it runs

Do: Point at each stage as it lights up — Ingest & georeferencing, Ground sample distance, Height estimation, Absolute calibration, GeoTIFF / PNG export, Textured 3D mesh.
While that runs — why this works at all.

A depth model trained on ordinary photographs predicts relative depth: what is nearer, what is further. It has no idea what a metre is. So we freeze it and train a small head on top, against airborne LiDAR, whose only job is turning that into metres above ground.
The obvious alternative is a metric depth model that outputs metres directly. We tried it. Those models assume a camera with a focal point, and a satellite orthomosaic has none — every pixel looks straight down. Its correlation with true height was 0.06. Effectively zero. The relative model reaches 0.41 before we train anything on top.
[OPTIONAL — cut this first if you are running long]
That single measurement is why the system is built this way. And it matches what the problem statement itself suggests: anchoring scale-agnostic depth to a coarse DEM. We use Copernicus GLO-30, fetched automatically, so absolute elevation comes out without the user supplying anything.
If asked later The head is 1.7 million trainable parameters on a frozen 97-million-parameter backbone, trained on 16,004 tiles — GAMUS plus Dutch AHN LiDAR at two resolutions. The shipped file is 6.6 MB.

4:15 – 5:15 · The numbers

Do: Click the Accuracy tab. Let the table fill the frame. Point at the mean row, then the per-landscape rows, then the resolutions.
Every number behind that comparison is in the app, not on a slide.

Eight held-out Dutch sites against airborne LiDAR: 5.32 metres RMSE on height above ground, correlation 0.67. The honest reference is predicting nothing — flat ground everywhere — and that is 8.37.

On the full absolute DSM, terrain plus structures, 6.11 — and half a metre of that is not the model, it is the offset between the global terrain datum and the local one. One control point removes it: 5.63.
The problem statement asks for stability across urban, sparse, hilly and forested terrain, so we measured each rather than hiding behind an average. Forest 2.24, farmland 1.56, hilly town 3.21, urban 8.40.

And evaluation uses Cartosat 2S at sixty centimetres — same model, same sites, imagery degraded to match: 5.35 at twenty-five centimetres, 5.51 at sixty, 5.72 at one metre. Flat across the range that matters.

5:15 – 6:00 · Limits, then close

Do: Open Example scenes → Veluwe — forest. Orbit gently while you finish.
One limitation, stated plainly, because you will see it if you look closely.

Tall structures come out short — roughly half their height on the tallest buildings, and canopy behaves the same way, which is why I am showing a forest. A single nadir photograph has no parallax, so when the model cannot tell a ten-metre building from a twenty-metre one, predicting something in between is mathematically right. We could scale it up to look better and the measured error would get worse. So we do not.

Ground and roads stay sub-metre. Buildings and trees carry almost all the rest, and we publish exactly how much.
All of this runs on a laptop from one command, CPU-only if there is no GPU. The trained head is six and a half megabytes, and every figure I quoted regenerates from a script in the repository.

One photograph in — a measured, explorable 3D surface out. Thank you.

Questions you may get, and short answers

QuestionAnswer
"How can one image give height?"The scale is not in the image — it is learned. The backbone gives relative ordering; the head converts that to metres using patterns learned from 16,004 LiDAR-measured tiles. Where the image is genuinely ambiguous we under-predict, and we publish that bias.
"Why not a model that outputs metres directly?"Tried it. Metric models assume a perspective camera; an orthomosaic has none. Correlation with true height 0.06 against 0.41 for relative depth.
"5.32 sounds large."It is dominated by tall structures. Ground and low vegetation are sub-metre. Predicting nothing scores 8.37. And no single-view method resolves a tower's height from one nadir look — we quantify the gap rather than hide it.
"Have you tested on ISRO imagery?"Not yet — we have no ISRO LiDAR to score against. What we have tested is resolution: 5.51 m at the 0.6 m Cartosat 2S resolution evaluation will use.
"Does it need a GPU?"No. CPU fallback is built in; run.sh detects the hardware and installs the right build.
"Is this reproducible?"Every number comes from a JSON file in the repo, and a script verifies the documentation against those files and fails if anything drifts.
