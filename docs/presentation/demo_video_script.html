0:00 – 0:35 · The problem

Do: Open on the app with Utrecht — suburban already loaded in the 3D view. Slowly orbit once while you talk. Do not click anything yet.
Elevation data is the first thing you want after a flood or an earthquake, and the first thing you do not have. A LiDAR survey is a commissioned flight — weeks and a budget. What you usually do have is a satellite photograph.

This is DepthWizard. One ordinary RGB image in; the surface in metres out — buildings, terrain, canopy — as a 3D scene you can fly through. No stereo pair, no LiDAR.
Do not say "AI-powered" or "state of the art". The people watching build satellites. Show them a measurement and they will trust you; use marketing words and they will not.

0:35 – 1:20 · Upload something real

Do: Click New processing job. Drag utrecht_suburb__UPLOAD_THIS.tif onto the drop zone. Let the thumbnail appear. Press Process image. Let the progress bar start moving.
This is a real aerial GeoTIFF of a suburb near Utrecht, 400 metres across at 25 centimetres per pixel. It is one of eight sites we hold out — the model has never seen it in training.

I am uploading it exactly as an analyst would. No preprocessing, no parameters. The file carries its own coordinate system, so the pipeline reads the scale from the image itself.
Do: While it runs, hover the Reference DEM and Ground control points fields so they are visible on camera, but leave them empty.
Both optional. Supply a local terrain model or surveyed control points and the output ties to your datum — one control point is worth about half a metre on absolute elevation. I am leaving them empty to show the default path: everything it needs, it fetches itself.

1:20 – 2:20 · How it works, while it runs

Do: Point at the live stage list as each one lights up — Ingest & georeferencing, Ground sample distance, Height estimation, Absolute calibration, Textured 3D mesh.
While that runs, the interesting part is why this works at all.

A depth model trained on ordinary photographs predicts relative depth — what is nearer, what is further. It has no idea what a metre is. So we freeze that model and train a small head on top of it, against real airborne LiDAR, whose only job is to turn relative depth into metres above ground.

The obvious alternative is a metric depth model that outputs metres directly. We tried it. Those models assume a camera with a focal point, and a satellite orthomosaic has none — every pixel looks straight down. Its correlation with true height was 0.06. Effectively zero. The relative model reaches 0.41 before we train anything on top.

That one measurement is why the system is built this way. And it matches what the problem statement itself suggests: anchoring scale-agnostic depth to a coarse DEM.
If the job finishes early, stop talking and move on. Do not stretch the explanation to fill time. A crisp video that ends at 5:20 beats a padded one at 6:30.

3:20 – 4:20 · The proof

This is the most important minute of the video. Anyone can show a plausible-looking 3D render. Almost nobody shows it next to the real measurement. Slow down here.
Do: Open Example scenes and click Utrecht — suburban, then toggle to its -truth twin. Flip between them two or three times, slowly, from the same camera angle.
This is the part I would want to see if I were evaluating this.

Our reconstruction, and airborne LiDAR of the same ground — the real measurement, same grid, same viewer. We ship the ground truth beside our own output so you can judge it rather than take my word for it.

Roads, gardens, the ground between the houses land within about twenty centimetres.

4:20 – 5:20 · The numbers

Do: Click the Accuracy tab. Let the table fill the frame. Point at the mean row, then at the per-landscape rows.
Every number behind that comparison is in the app, not in a slide.

Across eight held-out Dutch sites, scored against airborne LiDAR: 5.32 metres RMSE on height above ground, correlation 0.67. The honest reference point is predicting nothing at all — assuming flat ground everywhere — and that is 8.37 metres.

On the full absolute DSM, terrain plus structures, we are at 6.11 metres — and about half a metre of that is not our model at all. It is the constant offset between the global terrain datum and the local one. Supply a single ground control point and it drops to 5.63.

Ground and low vegetation, which are most of any image, come in at 0.82 and 0.86 metres.
Do: Point at the four landscape rows.
The problem statement asks for stability across urban, sparse, hilly and forested terrain, so we measured each separately rather than hiding behind one average. Forest 2.24, sparse farmland 1.56, hilly town 3.21, urban 8.40.
Do: Scroll to the resolution figures.
And because evaluation uses Cartosat 2S at sixty centimetres — the same model, on the same sites, with the imagery degraded to match. 5.35 metres at twenty-five centimetres, 5.51 at sixty, 5.72 at one metre. Essentially flat across the range that matters.

Past two metres it degrades, and by ten metres a building is one to three pixels across and there is nothing left to measure. We say so, and the pipeline warns you when the input is outside the range it was trained for.

5:20 – 6:00 · Limits, then close

Do: Go back to the 3D flythrough on a scene with tall buildings. Orbit gently while you finish.
One limitation, stated plainly, because you will see it if you look closely.

Tall structures come out short — roughly half their true height on the tallest buildings. A single nadir photograph contains no parallax, so when the model cannot tell a ten-metre building from a twenty-metre one, predicting something in between is mathematically the right answer. We could scale it up to look better and the measured error would get worse, so we do not.

Ground and roads stay sub-metre. Buildings and trees carry almost all the remaining error, and we publish exactly how much.
All of this runs on a laptop from one command, CPU-only if needed. The trained head is six and a half megabytes, and every figure I quoted regenerates from a script in the repository.

One photograph in — a measured, explorable 3D surface out. Thank you.

Delivery notes

  • Pace. 888 spoken words — 6 min 55 at a normal pace, 7 min 50 if you speak slowly. Read it once with a timer before recording. If you run long, the metric-model paragraph at 1:20 is the one to cut.
  • Mouse. Move deliberately. Hover a control for a beat before clicking it so the viewer's eye can follow.
  • The two moments that win it are the ground-truth toggle at 3:20 and the honest limitation at 5:20. Slow down for both. Volunteering a weakness reads as confidence, and evaluators notice.
  • If the live job fails on camera, do not restart it. Say "I have one already processed" and click it in Recent jobs. Recovering smoothly looks better than a clean take.
  • Do not show the terminal, the code, or the file system. Prototype only.
