0:00 – 0:50 · The problem

Do: Start with Washington DC — civic already open in the 3D flythrough. Orbit slowly all the way round while you talk. Do not click anything yet.
When a flood or an earthquake happens, almost every question that matters in the first few hours is a question about height. How deep does the water get, and which roads can a relief convoy use.

Answering that needs an elevation model, which is exactly what you usually do not have. Airborne LiDAR means commissioning a survey flight, and that takes weeks and a budget. But there is one thing you almost always do have within hours, and that is a single photograph from above.

Everything you are looking at here was reconstructed from one RGB image, with no stereo pair and no LiDAR. It is a real surface in real metres. Let me show you it working on something it has never seen.
Do not say "AI-powered", "cutting-edge" or "state of the art". The people watching build satellites for a living. Show them a measurement and they will trust you. Use marketing language and they will stop listening.

0:50 – 1:40 · Uploading something real

Do: Click New processing job. Drag valkenburg_hills__UPLOAD_THIS.tif onto the drop zone and pause on the thumbnail. Glance at the GSD override box to confirm it is empty. Press Process image.
This is a real aerial photograph of Valkenburg, a hill town in the south of the Netherlands. It covers four hundred metres of ground at twenty-five centimetres per pixel, and it is one of eight sites we deliberately kept out of training, so the model has never seen this place.

I am uploading it the way somebody actually working with this would, with no preprocessing and nothing tuned for this image. It is a GeoTIFF, so it carries its own coordinate system and the pipeline reads the ground scale straight out of the file.
Do: Hover over the Reference DEM and Ground control points fields so both are clearly on camera, then leave them empty.
Both of these are optional, and I mention them because the problem statement asks for them specifically. If you have a terrain model for the area, or even a handful of surveyed ground control points, you can supply them here, and a single control point is worth around half a metre of absolute accuracy.

I am leaving them empty to show what happens by default, which is that the system fetches everything it needs on its own. While that processes, let me explain why this approach works, because the obvious way of doing it does not.

1:40 – 3:10 · Why it works

Do: Point at each stage in the pipeline list as it lights up. Let the log scroll on its own. Do not click anything.
There are foundation models that predict depth from a single photograph very well, but what they predict is relative depth. They can tell you this is nearer than that, but not how far either actually is, because they were trained on ordinary photographs where absolute distance is not knowable.

So the real problem, which the problem statement calls scale calibration, is turning that into actual elevation. We freeze the depth model completely and train a small network on top whose only job is that conversion. It learns from sixteen thousand tiles of imagery paired with real LiDAR, and for every pixel it predicts a scale and an offset, because a rooftop and the street beside it need different corrections.
Now, there is an obvious alternative. Metric depth models output metres directly, so you could skip calibration entirely. We tried that first and it fails, and the reason is interesting.

Metric models assume they are looking through a camera with perspective, where things shrink with distance. A satellite orthomosaic has none of that, because every pixel looks straight down. When we measured how well a metric model correlated with real LiDAR height, we got zero point zero six, which is indistinguishable from noise. The relative model, before we trained anything on it, was already at zero point four one. That one measurement is why the whole system is built this way.
[CUT A — see the length ladder]
And it turns out that is also what the problem statement itself suggests, which is to take scale-agnostic depth features and anchor them to a lower resolution DEM. We use Copernicus GLO-30 and download it automatically, so absolute elevation comes out of the other end without the user having to supply anything at all.
Only if someone asks for specifics Frozen Depth Anything V2 Base at 97 million parameters. The trainable head is 1.7 million parameters and decodes the full feature pyramid. Trained on 16,004 tiles from GAMUS and Dutch AHN LiDAR at two ground resolutions. The loss combines scale-invariant log error, multi-scale gradient matching to keep roof edges sharp, an L1 anchor, and a term weighted by height. The shipped model file is 6.6 megabytes.

5:50 – 7:10 · The numbers

Do: Click the Accuracy tab and let the table fill the frame. Point at the mean row, then the landscape rows, then the resolution rows. Move the pointer slowly.
Every number behind that comparison is in the application itself, rather than on a slide.

We tested on eight sites across the Netherlands, all held out of training, scored against airborne LiDAR. On height above ground we get five point three two metres RMSE at a correlation of zero point six seven, against eight point three seven if you simply assumed the world was flat. That is the honest comparison.
This problem is scored on the absolute surface model, terrain and structures together, so I should give you that too: six point one one metres. About half a metre of that is not our model but a constant offset between the global terrain datum and the local one, and one ground control point removes it, taking us to five point six three.
The problem statement asks for stability across urban, sparse, hilly and forested landscapes, so we measured each separately rather than hiding behind an average. Forest two point two four, farmland one point five six, the hilly town I uploaded three point two one, and dense urban eight point four, which is the hardest case. The ground itself, most of the pixels in any image, comes in at eighty-two centimetres.

7:50 – 8:40 · What it cannot do, and closing

Do: Open Example scenes and click Veluwe — forest. Orbit gently and keep orbiting until you finish speaking.
Which brings me to the limitation, and I want to state it plainly because you will notice it yourself if you look closely.

Tall structures come out too short. On the tallest buildings we predict roughly half their true height, and tree canopy does the same, which is why I have deliberately put a forest on screen while I say this.

That is not a bug we have not got around to fixing. A photograph taken straight down contains no parallax, so when the model genuinely cannot tell a ten metre building from a twenty metre one, predicting something in between is the mathematically correct answer. We could multiply the tall predictions upwards so the render looked more impressive, and the measured error would get worse, so we do not. Ground, roads and low vegetation all stay under a metre, and we publish exactly how much of the error sits in buildings and canopy.
To close: all of this runs on an ordinary laptop from a single command, with a CPU-only path if there is no GPU. The trained model is six and a half megabytes, and every figure I have quoted regenerates from a script in the repository, including the ones that are not flattering.

One photograph goes in, and a measured three-dimensional surface you can explore comes out. Thank you for watching.

Delivery notes

  • Read it aloud once with a timer before recording. Anywhere a sentence feels awkward in your mouth, change the words. Sounding like yourself matters more than matching this script.
  • The three moments that carry the video are the metric-versus-relative measurement at 2:40, the reference toggle at 5:00, and the limitation at 7:50. Slow down for all three. Volunteering a weakness reads as confidence, and evaluators notice who is willing to do it.
  • The linking sentences matter. Lines like "which brings me to the numbers" and "but you should not believe any of it simply because it looks convincing" are what turn a tour of features into a single argument. Do not drop them.
  • Mouse discipline. Hover over a control for a beat before you click it, so the viewer's eye arrives before the screen changes. Avoid fast spins in the 3D view; they read as showing off and they compress badly on video.
  • If the live job fails while recording, do not restart it. Say "I have one already processed here" and click it in Recent jobs. Recovering smoothly looks better than a take where nothing went wrong.
  • Do not show the terminal, the source code, or your file system at any point.
  • If you need it shorter, use the length ladder near the top rather than trimming sentences as you go. Half-cut sentences are what make a read sound nervous.
  • If you finish early, let the flythrough run in silence for a few seconds. Quiet over a moving 3D scene looks confident; filler talk does not.

Questions you are likely to get afterwards

QuestionHow to answer it
"How can a single image give you height at all?"The scale is not in the image, it is learned. The backbone gives us relative ordering, and the head converts that into metres using patterns it learned across sixteen thousand LiDAR-measured tiles: shadow length, texture gradient, apparent size, surrounding context. Where the image is genuinely ambiguous we under-predict, and we publish that bias rather than hiding it.
"Why not just use a model that outputs metres directly?"We tried. Metric depth models assume a perspective camera and an orthomosaic does not have one. Their correlation with true height was 0.06, against 0.41 for a relative model before we trained anything on it.
"Five point three metres sounds like a lot."It is dominated by tall structures. Ground and low vegetation are both under a metre. Predicting nothing at all scores 8.37. And no single-view method resolves a tower's height from one look straight down, so we quantify that gap instead of hiding it.
"Have you tested on ISRO imagery?"Not yet, because we have no ISRO LiDAR to score against, and the FAQ confirms reference data will not be provided. What we have tested is resolution, and at the 0.6 m Cartosat 2S resolution that evaluation will use we get 5.51 m.
"What happens on coarser imagery than 2 m?"It degrades, and we say so. By 10 m a building spans one to three pixels and there is nothing left to infer from. The pipeline warns the user when the input falls outside the range it was trained for.
"Does it need a GPU?"No. There is a CPU fallback built in, and run.sh detects the hardware and installs the matching build.
"Is any of this reproducible?"Every number comes from a JSON file in the repository, and there is a script that checks the documentation against those files and fails if anything has drifted.
"What about licensing?"The default backbone is CC-BY-NC, which is fine for evaluation and academic use but not for commercial deployment. We measured a permissively licensed alternative as statistically equivalent, and it is documented.
